The Ancient Art of Water Gilding: Armenian Bole to Agate Burnishing

A step-by-step masterclass inside our gilding bench, from cooking rabbit-skin glue gesso to laying 22k Italian gold leaf.

Julian Vance, GCF Master Guilder & Joinery Director

  • ⏱️ 10 Min Read

Water gilding is one of the oldest decorative techniques in the world, practised in much the same way since the Italian Renaissance. It is slow, unforgiving and entirely by hand, and it produces a mirror-bright gold that no paint or foil can imitate.

A Craft Older Than the Renaissance

Cennino Cennini described the process in his 15th-century handbook Il Libro dell'Arte, and the materials have barely changed: animal-skin glue, chalk, clay and pure gold. Many of the frames surrounding Old Master paintings in today's museums were gilded this way.

Step 1: Preparing the Gesso

Rabbit-skin glue granules are soaked overnight, then gently warmed and mixed with fine whiting (calcium carbonate). The warm gesso is brushed onto the carved wood in eight to twelve thin coats, each one applied before the last has fully dried.

Once cured, the gesso is sanded and recut with fine tools to restore the crispness of the carving. This stage alone can take several days on an ornate frame.

Step 2: Applying the Armenian Bole

Bole is a fine clay mixed with glue. It provides a smooth, slightly cushioned surface for the gold and gives the gilding its underlying warmth. Traditional colours include:

  • Red bole: warm, rich gold, classic for Italian and French frames.
  • Yellow bole: applied in recesses so worn areas look natural.
  • Grey or black bole: cooler tones, often used beneath white gold or silver.

Step 3: Laying the Gold Leaf

The bole is wetted with "gilder's liquor", a mixture of water and a little alcohol, which reactivates the glue. A sheet of 22k gold leaf, about 1/8000th of a millimetre thick, is lifted with a squirrel-hair gilder's tip and floated onto the wet surface. Capillary action pulls it flat instantly.

  1. Cut the leaf on a suede gilder's cushion with a straight-edged knife.
  2. Charge the gilder's tip with static by brushing it against your cheek or forearm.
  3. Wet a small area, lay the leaf, and move on before the water dries.
  4. Overlap each sheet slightly to avoid "holidays" (gaps).

Step 4: Agate Burnishing

After the leaf has dried to just the right moisture, the gilder polishes selected areas with a polished agate stone. The pressure compresses the gold onto the bole, creating a brilliant, mirror-like shine. Areas left unburnished stay soft and matte, and that contrast gives carved frames their depth.

“Burnishing is a conversation with the gesso. Too early and you tear the leaf; too late and it will never shine.”

— Julian Vance, Master Guilder
🏛️
Restoration Note

Never clean gilded frames with water or household sprays; water gilding is water-soluble by design. A soft, dry sable brush is all that is needed for routine dusting.

Step 5: Toning & Patination

New gold can look brash beside an antique painting. We finish with delicate toning: rottenstone, pigmented shellac and careful distressing on high points. The aim is a frame that looks loved, not new, and sits in harmony with the artwork it surrounds.
