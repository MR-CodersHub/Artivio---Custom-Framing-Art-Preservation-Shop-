Why Tru Vue® Museum Glass is the Gold Standard for Fine Art Protection

Ultraviolet radiation is irreversible: once light bleaches organic watercolor pigments or yellows wood-pulp rag, no conservation process can recreate the lost tonal values. Here is why the glazing you choose matters more than any other framing decision.

Dr. Alistair Chen Senior Paper Conservator

  • ⏱️ 8 Min Read

Most people choose a frame for its moulding. Conservators look at the glass first. The glazing is the only barrier between a work of art and the light, dust and pollutants that slowly break it down. A beautiful hardwood profile paired with ordinary picture glass is, from a preservation standpoint, a beautiful way to watch a print fade.

The Invisible Threat of Light Damage

Light damage is cumulative and permanent. Every hour of exposure adds to a lifetime "dose" that a pigment can tolerate before it visibly shifts. Ultraviolet wavelengths (300–400 nm) carry the most energy and do the most harm. They break the molecular bonds in dyes, inks and paper fibres, causing fading, yellowing and brittleness.

Standard float glass filters only about 45% of UV radiation. That means more than half of the most damaging light still reaches the artwork, every single day it hangs on a sunlit wall.

“Fading is not an accident that happens to art. It is a slow chemical reaction, and glazing is the single most effective way to slow it down.”

— Dr. Alistair Chen, Senior Paper Conservator

How Museum Glass Works

Tru Vue® Museum Glass combines two technologies in a single pane:

  • UV-filtering coating: blocks up to 99% of UV radiation in the 300–380 nm range.
  • Magnetron-sputtered anti-reflective layers: microscopically thin metal-oxide films deposited in a vacuum chamber that cancel reflected light through destructive interference.
  • Neutral colour transmission: over 97% light transmission with no green cast, so whites stay white.

The result is glass that seems to disappear. Viewers often reach out to touch the artwork, surprised to find a pane in front of it.

Which Artworks Need It Most

Every original deserves UV protection, but some media are especially vulnerable:

  1. Watercolors and gouache: organic pigments like alizarin crimson and indigo can fade noticeably within a few years.
  2. Vintage photographs: chromogenic prints shift towards magenta or cyan as their dyes break down.
  3. Textiles and signed memorabilia: silk, wool and felt-tip signatures are among the most light-sensitive materials in a collection.
  4. Works on newsprint or wood-pulp paper: lignin oxidises and yellows rapidly under UV exposure.
💡
Conservator's Tip

UV glazing slows fading but does not stop visible light. Even behind Museum Glass, avoid hanging sensitive works opposite a south-facing window, and rotate delicate pieces every few seasons.

Is It Worth the Investment?

Museum Glass typically adds 25–40% to the cost of a custom frame. Compared with the cost of professional restoration, or the irreversible loss of an heirloom, it is the most cost-effective preservation decision a collector can make. It also transforms the viewing experience: colours look richer, blacks look deeper, and the frame finally feels like a window rather than a mirror.

If you are unsure which glazing suits your piece, bring it to the studio. We will demonstrate each option side by side under gallery lighting so you can see the difference for yourself.
