Preserving Textile Heirlooms: Why You Must Never Glue Fabric

The devastating effects of spray adhesives on vintage silk, lace and military uniforms, and the conservation stitching method we use instead.

Dr. Alistair Chen Senior Paper Conservator
  • 📅 July 22, 2026
  • ⏱️ 7 Min Read
📑 In this article
  1. What Adhesives Do to Fabric
  2. The Conservation Stitching Method
  3. Choosing the Right Shadowbox Depth
  4. Protecting Textiles on Display

Christening gowns, wedding lace, regimental flags, sports jerseys: textiles carry some of a family's most precious stories. Sadly, they are also among the objects most often damaged by well-meaning framing. The most common culprit is a can of spray adhesive.

What Adhesives Do to Fabric

Spray mounts and hot-glue guns seem like an easy way to keep fabric flat, but they cause lasting harm:

  • Staining: solvents migrate through fibres and yellow over time, often showing through to the front.
  • Embrittlement: adhesives harden as they age, cracking silk and fine linen along fold lines.
  • Irreversibility: once glued, a textile usually cannot be removed without tearing.

“Every treatment we apply must be reversible. If a future conservator cannot undo our work without harm, we should not have done it.”

— Dr. Alistair Chen

The Conservation Stitching Method

Instead of glue, we use the method followed by museum textile departments. The fabric is supported on a padded, fabric-covered board and secured with hundreds of tiny stitches that spread its weight evenly.

  1. A board of 100% cotton rag is padded with polyester batting and wrapped in washed, undyed cotton or Belgian linen.
  2. The textile is positioned and gently aligned along its natural grain.
  3. Using fine curved needles and colour-matched silk or polyester thread, we stitch through the weave, never through embroidery or beading.
  4. Stitches pass between threads rather than piercing them, so they can be snipped out years later without a trace.

Choosing the Right Shadowbox Depth

Textiles should never touch the glazing. Contact traps moisture and can transfer fibres to the glass. Spacers or a deep shadowbox keep an air gap of at least 6 mm, and more for dimensional objects.

ItemSuggested Interior DepthMounting Method
Lace, handkerchiefs15–25 mmStitched to padded linen
Sports jerseys40–60 mmStitched over a shaped insert
Uniforms with medals60–90 mmStitched + custom medal bars
Flags25–40 mmFull-surface stitched support
🧵
Conservator's Tip

Do not wash or iron an heirloom textile before framing. Old fibres can shrink or tear. Bring it to us as it is, and we will assess whether gentle surface cleaning is safe.

Protecting Textiles on Display

Textile dyes are extremely light-sensitive. We always glaze textiles with 99% UV-filtering glass or acrylic and advise hanging them away from direct sunlight, radiators and bathrooms. With the right mount and environment, a framed heirloom can remain vibrant for generations.
